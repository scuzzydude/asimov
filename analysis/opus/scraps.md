# Scraps from the Floor: *Asimov*

**Source:** v0.01 through v2.13
**Prepared by:** Opus analysis pass
**Method:** Identify deleted blocks of ≥5 lines with standalone value (analogy, anecdote, joke, worked example, concept) that do not reappear elsewhere in any subsequent version.

A "scrap" here is content Brandon wrote, kept for one or more versions, and then deleted without putting it back. It does not include rewrites where the same idea appears in different prose. It does not include placeholder notes ("...netflix...Rembrandt") or fragments that were obviously not meant to be permanent.

A note on the relationship between scraps and the larger pattern: Brandon's revision pattern is `#if 0` rather than delete. Most "removed" content actually persists — sometimes as a footnote in a later version, sometimes as a parenthetical, sometimes rewritten as a different kind of section (the Royal Treasure scrap below was reborn as a fake encyclopedist's redaction stamp 500 years in the future, which is more interesting than the original prose). The scraps below are the genuine kills — content that left the book and did not come back.

## SCRAP: v1.05 → v1.63 — Frank's Bikes (the worked example)

> Frank's Bikes makes basic low-cost bicycles and sells them through the developing world. Frank instantiates this idea as a Scoot, and in his charter, he limits the markup of the bicycles to 10%. Labor costs are kept low because final assembly is done by local labor, who are members of his scootage. Frank expands throughout the world, selling millions of bicycles. Frank's supply chain is global, and he keeps cost low by buying in very large quantities and giving his suppliers many months of lead time. Under normal circumstances, Frank wouldn't consider his inventory of raw materials a property of wealth, as they all directly go towards the appreciation of his Scoot — low-cost green transportation for people that need it. A series of natural disasters and labor disputes interrupt the supply of rubber, just after Frank took delivery of an entire shipload of tires and innertubes that under normal circumstances would last him an entire year. For-profit bicycle makers offer Frank up to five times his cost for the rubber — this would allow Frank to effectively subsidize the cost of the rest of the materials and allow Frank's Bikes to sell the bikes for the cost of the labor alone. Because it's used for arbitrage, the rubber becomes a property of wealth, and despite the appreciation of the original idea, it would not be allowed.

**Why it matters:** This is the manuscript's clearest worked example of "Property of Wealth (POW)." It shows how a Scoot's mission-aligned inventory can flip into a forbidden category through arbitrage opportunity, and why that flip matters for the responsibility-domain/value-domain separation. Without Frank's Bikes, the POW concept in v2.13 has to be carried by the Picasso example alone, which works for art but not for productive enterprise.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **High.** This is engineering documentation in narrative form. The Scoot Primer in v2.06 added a lot of structural definition but never replaced this worked example. A modern reader trying to instantiate a Scoot for a productive enterprise would benefit directly from Frank's Bikes. It could return verbatim as "Worked Example: Frank's Bikes (Property of Wealth, edge case)" in a future appendix.

## SCRAP: v1.05 → v1.63 — Scoot of the Crown / Diamond Pooper-Scooper

> Take everything that is leftover, the personal residences of the Queen and her offspring, the summer homes, her personal jewelry, diamond encrusted pooper-scooper, and create a Scoot of Crown (SOC). The ISO will be tendered to the British people who want to be responsible for the royal family. There are plenty of Americans who prefer the Windsor to the Kardashian, so you could open the Scoot globally. When the SOC runs out of money, the Queen must start signing autographs or collecting speaking fees, or the scoot could be diluted with another sale. Maybe Parliament is charged £1000 each time the PM stops by for tea.
>
> In any case, the Crown can exist as long there are people willing to be responsible for it. It's a scoot of the *Crown* so maybe the current monarch serves as the scoot trustee and has the duty to preserve the history of genius of the kings and queens of England. Many of them did some great things. If you've watched the historically accurate docuseries *The Tudors*, you would know King Henry VIII was the inventor of the modern-day divorce industry. Maybe awarding a knighthood becomes the Nobel prize for divorce lawyers.

**Why it matters:** This was a complete worked example of how a national-treasure Scoot could fund itself through dilution and earned income (autographs, speaking fees, PM tea charges). The "diamond encrusted pooper-scooper" line is one of the funniest specific images in the book; "Henry VIII as inventor of the modern-day divorce industry" is in the top tier of Brandon's compressed jokes. The British Royal Family was, for many versions, the structural example of how to handle historical-vanity capital — replacing it with a Picasso left a gap in the rhetorical map.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **High.** The umbrella section "Royal Treasure" was rewritten in v2.13 as a fake "Historical_Rewrite_Redaction" stamp signed by future Encyclopedist `hakeemala17` in the year 2522. That replacement is brilliant — but it deletes all the worked-example mechanics. There is room to restore the prose as a footnote or sidebar inside the redaction frame, where it would function as the redacted content the future Encyclopedist is overwriting. Brandon's preservation principle (`#if 0`, never delete) suggests he would approve.

## SCRAP: v0.02 → v0.03 — The Original "Origins" Chapter (long-form personal narrative)

> From the beginning of the pandemic, I'd been developing an idea with a friend that was a replacement for banks and the financial industry for businesses. Fundamentally, it used dynamic scalability for economies of scale. My model was what Amazon did for IT with AWS. I had quite a bit of reading of economic history as research.
>
> For several years, my son had been trying to get me to invest in crypto, and as a coder, I understood bitcoin but was fundamentally skeptical. I certainly liked the decentralization aspect; I love solutions to problems that don't involve government. One the other hand, I had issue with the arbitrary creation of perceived value... I am not an idealist when it comes to my own money — I wished I had listened to my son years ago. I think bitcoin was around \$300 when he first implored me to invest.
>
> Coincidently, I'd been having long discussions with my daughter about all the social and political craziness we had to deal with in 2020 with the elections, protests and the pandemic. I blamed social media for of the hyperbole and hysteria. I also realized my generation had invented the technology that enabled all this but had not addressed the ethical problems that technology created. One of the core problems that was behind everything was wealth inequality. My attitude about this can be best summed up by Pink Floyd, "Money, it's a crime, share it fairly, but don't take a slice of my pie." I'm all for voluntary collectivism, spread the wealth, but I don't trust the government to distribute it...
>
> After the first draft of the business idea, my friend implored me to add a crypto play. I was hesitant, as I didn't want to debase the fundamental idea. There was one technical problem with my system, and as it turned out, crypto provided an elegant solution. In early 2021 I'd been doing a lot of study of crypto from a technical perspective. While I was impressed with the technology, I became even more skeptical of its societal value. About this time, my wife joined my son in the crypto-craze, and proceeded to multiply her initial investment by a hundredfold on paper. I'd just turned fifty, so it was as if the universe was telling me, "Give up already, you're old."

**Why it matters:** This is the only place in the manuscript where Brandon narrates the chronological origin of the project as a continuous personal story — pandemic, son pushing crypto, daughter's discussions, wife's hundredfold gain on paper, age fifty. The Pink Floyd quote ("Money, it's a crime") survives in v2.13's "Origins" chapter, but the longer arc of the family's role in pushing him toward this project does not. The 1.05+ "Origins" chapter is shorter and more abstract.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **Medium-high.** The personal narrative voice in this scrap is what the early manuscript was missing and what eventually arrived in the Steve Fairchild section. It could be re-inserted at the start of "Origins" as a "before this book existed" frame.

## SCRAP: v0.02 → v0.03 — The Mountain Sermon Riff (Ten Commandments anecdote)

> Today our decisions about our politics, investments, social morality and our environment are directly influenced by the random broadcast thoughts of billionaires and celebrities. This is our reality? Can you imagine if Nikola Tesla's contemporary and compatriot, Mark Twain, had this level of source material to work with? That level of biting satire could have taken us directly from robber barons to Lennon's *Imagine*-land, skipping all the industrial warfare and bloody revolutions, saving a billion lives in the process...
>
> It used to be, for a leader to have moral authority, he had to do something substantially beneficial for people to take him seriously. Dude come down from a mountain, got him a list of ten rules he wants you to follow, your wife's all like, "Why the hell should we listen to him." And you're like, "Remember, babe, last year we were starving, getting whipped all the time, having to haul those huge ass stones all over the place for that dude with the big head, wears makeup all the time. Remember that?" She's like, "Well, I like that Sabbath thing, I could use a day off."

**Why it matters:** A perfect specimen of what I've been calling Brandon's Register 4 (Comedian-Absurdist) operating in service of a serious analytical point. The dialog about the Ten Commandments is one of the funnier passages in any version of the manuscript and it carries an actual argument: moral authority used to require demonstrable benefit, now it requires only access to a megaphone. This is the kind of thing that distinguishes Brandon from a typical crypto whitepaper author; it was cut early before the book learned it was allowed to be funny in this register.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **High.** Could restore as either an "Ethical Revolution" interlude or as a footnote to one of the Mark Twain / Nikola Tesla passages. Loses nothing, gains comedic and rhetorical force.

## SCRAP: v0.02 → v0.03 — Mars as the Old West / Property Wars in Space

> A system that values genius is fundamentally sound not only for our peaceful coexistence with each other, but our coexistence with nature... Wait a minute? Who's going to grow rice, and mow lawns, and paint my nails, if all the little ones spend all their time trying to be little geniuses?
>
> Super-efficient robots that are powered by green energy. Don't believe me? Ask Elon Musk. How else are we going to colonize Mars? Pretty stupid to have super-robots on Mars and not use them here. Mars is a dangerous place. So is the earth for people forced by circumstance of birth to lives of brutally hard physical labor.
>
> I think that's what all these crazy tweets are about. I know Mr. Musk reads science fiction, and surely he's read at least one story where the children of the rich manage to escape the dying earth and go to Mars, or a space-station, or another star system, while the masses just float around on the ocean, starving, hoping a good looking dumbass farmer on a jet-ski will find them and take them to dry land. You think you're going to launch those rich kids from Texas? I'm not actually a big sci-fi geek, I don't keep up with everything, but I am almost sure there is a genre of "Mars as the Old West" or "Mars as a land of roaming samurai." If not, I hereby exert exclusive rights to all work in this genre, royalty negotiable. Yeah, you think we won't have property wars on Mars because we're only going to send nice people to Mars? I sure hope the water is evenly distributed.

**Why it matters:** A unique extended take on Musk that doesn't survive into later versions. The "good looking dumbass farmer on a jet-ski" is a specific image (a *Waterworld* reference) that does work the rest of the book doesn't do — pop culture as the receptacle of obvious dystopian futures we can't otherwise discuss seriously. The conclusion ("If you take the value system that we currently use on Earth to space you will also take war to space") survives in the Second Foundation Trantor passage in v2.13, but the supporting arc — the working-out of what happens when the rich leave — does not.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **Medium.** Brandon's later Musk treatment is more glancing ("Back off, Musk, it's not you"). This longer take might feel out of sync with the final book's tone. Could survive as a footnote at the Trantor passage.

## SCRAP: v0.02 → v0.03 — Brandon's Hypocrisy Confession (the dinner-party paragraph)

> Let's say I'm at a dinner party with a bunch of fancy artsy people. And some rich dirtbag makes mention he's got a Picasso hidden away at his ski-chalet in Aspen, and only the few other rich dirtbags invited on his ski trip ever get to see it. My response would be "Should I bow or curtsey, your royal fucking highness?"

**Why it matters:** A clean specimen of Brandon's moral vocabulary in compressed form — "rich dirtbag", "ski-chalet in Aspen", "your royal fucking highness". The full-strength profanity is interesting because v2.13's Brandon has been carefully cleaned of "fucking" outside of song lyrics. This passage shows the natural register before EdA and EdB shaped it.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **Low.** The thought survives elsewhere in cleaner form ("If you have another point of view... then you should stop reading"). The profanity makes the passage harder to place in the final book's register, even though it's funnier in the original.

## SCRAP: v0.51 → v0.58 — Joke-Placeholder SEC Footnotes

The early versions contained footnotes that were placeholders for what eventually became the SEC vs. Ripple Labs analysis. Sample placeholder language from v0.51:

> [^X] *(joke about SEC v Howey or whatever, fill in later)*
> *...Need legal review here, but the point is scoot trades responsibility, not property...*

These placeholders existed across multiple versions in v0.5x. They were systematically replaced — not deleted, but transformed — in the v0.85 → v1.05 era when the actual Ripple Labs case became central enough to demand serious citation.

**Why it matters:** Documents Brandon's working method. He wrote the joke first, knew it was a placeholder, kept it until the real reasoning crystallized, and then replaced it with the citation. **[CODE-PARALLEL]** This is exactly the `// TODO: implement properly` pattern in C code — keep the comment that declares intent, and replace the body when the implementation is ready. In code, you'd never delete the TODO; you'd convert it to a real function. Brandon does the same thing in prose.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **Low** (as deletions) — the replacements are better than the originals. **High as documentation of method** — they show that the manuscript was written engineering-first.

## SCRAP: v0.02 → v0.03 — "We bow to no man, only to our creator" Religious Frame

> Our common shared value is that all men are created equal. No man by right of birth is better than another. We bow to no man, only to our creator.
>
> Through the lens of history, we see that basic principle was hypocritical in practice, a perfect ideal tarnished by slavery, the genocide of indigenous people, and the denial of fundamental rights to our own mothers and daughters. Why is that?
>
> A failure of the American Revolution, and all the democratic revolutions that followed, was that we said to the king, "We don't need you," but "We still need your gold." And that fact is why we, as a nation, could rectify slavery and genocide, economic imperialism and decade after decade of bloody industrial savagery with the idealism of liberty and freedom. All men are created equal, but from the day he is born, we value a person's worth by the same king's gold. We might have hacked the actual gold out of the algorithm, but it is still the king's gold be it fiat or crypto.

**Why it matters:** This is the *original framing* of the "king's gold" concept that becomes load-bearing in the final book. The earliest version included an explicit religious frame ("We bow to no man, only to our creator") that the final book systematically removes. The argument retained — "we said to the king 'we don't need you' but 'we still need your gold'" — is one of the most quoted concepts in v2.13. But the religious framing has been completely cleaned out.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **Medium.** The religious frame is contentious; Brandon clearly decided the argument worked better without it. But the extended historical arc (American Revolution → bloody industrial savagery → king's gold persists) is sharper here than in any later version.

## SCRAP: v0.02 → v0.03 — "I'd be a hypocrite to say a system based on artwork would be valuable to me"

> While this system works with physical artwork, I'd be a hypocrite to say a system based on artwork would be valuable to me. The congruency between what is valuable to me and what is valuable as artwork is genius and vanity. The geniuses I admire in this narrative are all people I'm pledging to create scoot for, though the path for that isn't as clear as with physical artwork.

**Why it matters:** A rare moment of clean self-disclosure that doesn't survive into later versions. Brandon admitting, on the page, that physical-artwork Scoot is not what motivates him personally — it's the conceptual extension to athletes, musicians, mentors, drummers, etc. that makes the system feel valuable. The final book makes this point through its analogy choices but never says it outright.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **Medium.** Could function as a methodological aside before the "Immaterial Genius" pivot.

## SCRAP: v0.02 → v0.03 — Initial Clone Offering (ICO) Precise Definition

The footnote definition of ICO in v0.02:

> [^19]: A mid-21^st^ century trend where basekball fans would get toghether and issue and ICO (Intial Clone Offering) where they would mix the DNA from various superstars to create NBA players. Stephan Currys DNA was present in all NBA players, so the kids had taken to calling them all currys.

Survives in v2.13 in revised form as footnote 126, but the original v0.02 phrasing is more compressed and more absurdist: "Stephan Currys DNA was present in all NBA players" is a stronger absurdist claim than v2.13's softened "Stephan Curry's DNA was present to some extent in almost every clone."

**Why it matters:** This is a small one but it documents a specific kind of editorial softening — the unmarked claim ("present in all NBA players") replaced with the hedge ("present to some extent in almost every clone"). The v0.02 version is funnier because it's more total. This is EdB at work, but applied to a footnote, where Brandon's voice usually wins.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **Low.** Both versions exist; the original is preserved in git.

## SCRAP: v0.02 → v0.03 — "Saw the Worm in Korea" Dialog Closer (No Small Genius)

The earliest "No Small Genius" chapter ended with this exchange:

> "Yo, old man, why you care about rebounding so much? My curry never misses?"
>
> "Kid, let me tell you about the Worm. Back when I was a kid, the Korean peninsula was divided into two countries. There was this kid from Texas, like you and me…."

**Why it matters:** This is the original cliffhanger ending of the "No Small Genius" chapter — the old man pivoting from his great-musician memory to launching into the Dennis Rodman / Kim Jong Il story. The cut-off mid-sentence ("…") was a deliberate dramatic device. The v2.13 ending is different: the No Small Genius chapter no longer ends here, and the Rodman story is told in full elsewhere. The original was sharper as a chapter closer.

**Rehabilitation potential:** **Medium.** The chapter ending could return as either an alternate-ending epilogue or as a structural callback near the Rodman section. The cliffhanger is a stronger close than what v2.13 currently has.

## Most Worth Rehabilitating

In order of how much I would advocate restoring:

1. **Frank's Bikes** — engineering-grade worked example with no equivalent in v2.13. (High-confidence reinstatement)
2. **Scoot of the Crown / pooper-scooper** — pure comedic-analytical density. The current Royal Treasure redaction frame can absorb it as an embedded artifact.
3. **The Mountain Sermon riff (Ten Commandments dialog)** — best register-4 specimen cut from the book. Either restore or recompose.
4. **Texas Catfish scaffold notes** — restore "Chasing whales up a river of mud" as a regulatory metaphor.
5. **The Original Origins narrative** — at least one paragraph should return as an "before this book existed" frame.

The rest are interesting documentation of the manuscript's evolution but do not need to come back. They are the `#if 0` blocks — better preserved in git than restored to the running build.

# Voice Profile: Brandon Awbrey — "Write Like Brandon"

**Source:** *Asimov* manuscript, v0.2–v2.13 (91 tagged versions)
**Prepared by:** Opus analysis pass

---

## 1. Sentence Architecture

Brandon writes in two distinct rhythms that alternate rapidly, sometimes within the same paragraph. The short mode is almost percussive — declarative sentences of five to twelve words, delivered one after another with no connecting tissue. The long mode is a digression that sprawls through subordinate clauses, em-dashes, and parentheticals before eventually landing on a punch.

**What triggers short mode:** Making a point land. Thesis statements. Transitions between ideas. Lines he wants to be quotable. Moments of contempt or disgust. Anything that functions as a joke punchline.

**What triggers long mode:** Working through an analogy in real time. Personal anecdote. Historical or technical exposition. Moments when he is genuinely uncertain and wants the reader to follow his reasoning rather than just accept the conclusion.

**The transition pattern:** He will often build toward a long digression, let it run three to five sentences, and then snap it closed with a one-line declarative that acts as a verdict:

> *"Thomas Jefferson authored the blueprint for the true global revolution. Revolution burned like wildfire and the feudal powers were gone or sterilized in two hundred years. It only took two hundred years. It was the status quo of governance since the dawn of civilization, five-thousand years at least."*
> 
> That trailing line — five words. Verdict delivered.

He also opens chapters with stripped-down declaratives before expanding:

> *"There are only two true hedges against inflation."*

One sentence. No warm-up. The argument is already underway on line one.

The architecture mirrors his C coding style: brief function signatures followed by implementation bodies. He sets the return type first, then writes the work.

## 2. Vocabulary Fingerprint

**At least 25 characteristic words and phrases:**

1. **"genius"** — the load-bearing noun of the entire book. Used in every register from philosophical to casual. Appears hundreds of times; never feels worn out because he treats it as a technical term with a precise definition.

2. **"vanity"** — always twinned with genius. The pair functions like a binary enum: ideas are one or the other, and only history can tell you which.

3. **"property"** / **"the king's gold"** — his shorthand for the entire property-based value system he is arguing against. "The king's gold" appears when he wants contempt; "property" appears when he wants precision.

4. **"Scoot"** / **"scoot"** — his primary technical term, but he deploys it with the confidence of someone who invented the word and knows everyone else will eventually adopt it.

5. **"responsibility"** — not a platitude. He treats it as an engineering term: measurable, assignable, verifiable. "With great responsibility comes great wealth" is his explicit inversion of Gates.

6. **"recursive integration"** / **"karmic integration"** — the coding concept he extends to history, society, and personal ethics. When he uses "recursive integration" in prose, he is writing a comment block explaining a function he has already written in C.

7. **"y'all"** — deployed at key moments of direct address, usually when he wants to signal that the preceding technical section is done and the real talk is starting. "Scoot is money, y'all."

8. **"dude"** — conversational, not pejorative. Almost always in dialogue or anecdote; signals that we have exited the manifesto register and are now in scene.

9. **"bad-ass"** — reserved for genuine admiration. Bill Gates is "the most bad-ass businessman and coder in human history." Not used casually.

10. **"The Dream"** — Hakeem Olajuwon. Never named directly in the first mention; assumed knowledge. The highest term of genius-admiration in the book.

11. **"Clutch City"** / **"Choke City"** — Houston Rockets identity markers. Used to explain how communities own their own narrative.

12. **"sports karma"** — a recurring concept, treated with the same analytical rigor as recursive integration. Updated in footnotes with timestamps as karma is confirmed.

13. **"Queen Bey"** — Beyoncé. Elevated language for an elevated figure. He is from Houston; she is from Houston; the royalty metaphor is not ironic.

14. **"circular logic"** — used without apology. He acknowledges his argument is circular and then explains why that's a feature, not a bug.

15. **"GOAT debate"** — sports radio shorthand used to analogize philosophical questions about historical value. The GOAT debate is his model for how genius gets measured.

16. **"hard-ass"** / **"bastard"** — applied to antagonists or obstacles; sparser than "bad-ass." The contrast is intentional.

17. **"Marsellus Wallace"** — a specific pop-culture citation delivered without context, like a footnote reference you're expected to know. From *Pulp Fiction*, "things would be getting medieval up in here."

18. **"the little people"** — used without condescension; he means the non-billionaires who this system is supposedly built for, and he includes himself in the category.

19. **"data is the fuel of system designs"** — his engineering credo, usually deployed when dismissing vague argument in favor of specific measurement.

20. **"Crap"** — mild profanity, appears in dialogue or internal monologue, signals a pivot or a sudden recognition of a problem. "Crap, what do we do?"

21. **"Look"** — paragraph opener that signals he is about to re-explain something in simpler terms. Also signals mild impatience.

22. **"methodology"** — his highest engineering value, inherited directly from Steve Fairchild. "The methodology is the most important thing."

23. **"digital stone"** — his term for blockchain immutability. Poetic and precise simultaneously. Used consistently from early versions onward.

24. **"wins above replacement"** — borrowed from baseball analytics, deployed for historical figures. "What was Jefferson's wins above replacement?" is a genuine analytical question for him.

25. **"fullness"** / **"fair"** / **"equitable"** — his equity vocabulary. He distinguishes between equal (same amount) and equitable (proportional to contribution or need). The precision is deliberate and consistent.

26. **"Hallowed Grounds of Lakewood Church in Houston, Texas"** — his signature move: applying the most reverent language to the most irreverent subject. Houston is always treated with the vocabulary of the sacred.

27. **"Scootage"** — his portmanteau for the community of scoot holders. Invented terminology handled with full confidence.

## 3. Analogy Domain Map

Brandon draws his analogies from a fixed repertoire of five domains. He does not reach randomly — each domain serves a specific rhetorical function.

### Sports (Primary Domain — Houston-Specific)

**Examples:** Hakeem Olajuwon ("The Dream"), the 1994–95 Rockets championship, James Harden's departure, Karl Malone and John Stockton as villain archetypes, "sports karma" as a recurring analytical framework, the Toyota Center as vanity example, Clutch City vs. Choke City.

**Why he reaches here:** Sports is where he has seen genius measured publicly, repeatedly, with good data. The NBA has box scores. Sports karma is observable. The GOAT debate is the civilian version of the genius valuation problem he is trying to solve. Houston sports is his default emotional register — the lens through which he experiences triumph, betrayal, and community.

**Specific tic:** He uses Hakeem as the ultimate standard of genius-without-vanity, and Harden as the counter-example of vanity-without-follow-through. The pairing is structural, not incidental.

### C Code and Engineering (Structural Domain)

**Examples:** Recursive Integration as a named process extending his C design philosophy; the assembly-line tire installer as a parallelism for distributed computing ("Replace 1 CPU with 4 CPUs"); "data is the fuel of system designs"; methodology as the most important thing (Steve Fairchild's lesson); the vtable metaphor implicit in his argument that callers never change, only implementations swap.

**Why he reaches here:** His own professional identity. When he needs to explain something *rigorously*, he reaches for engineering language. When he needs to explain something *simply*, he uses an engineering worked example. The code domain is where he is most confident that he is right and everyone else is measuring wrong.

### Science Fiction (Metaphysical Domain)

**Examples:** *Foundation* / Hari Seldon as the central organizing metaphor; *Die Hard 7* as his invented thought experiment; Da Vinci appears as a character in Bruce Willis's dream sequence; Zoey's narrative interlude uses science fiction as allegory; the "Mule" character from Asimov deployed as a real-world wildcard analysis.

**Why he reaches here:** Science fiction is where he thinks. Asimov is the model he is trying to emulate — writing a book that is simultaneously wild speculation and historical prediction. He invents Die Hard 7 not as a joke but as a serious policy thought experiment with a cast of named characters.

### Houston Geography and Texas History (Identity Domain)

**Examples:** The Brazos River catfish story; the Toyota Center; the Juicebox (Minute Maid Park); downtown H-Town on August 2nd; Lakewood Church; the Texas country folk wisdom about avoiding crazy people downtown; the Appendix E Texas History section covering "Big Mama," "Little Mama," "Stinky Fingers," and "The Canadian."

**Why he reaches here:** Texas is not a place to him — it is a value system. The catfish story is not local color; it is a parable about the epistemology of history. Grandpa's rule — "when you don't have all the facts, history is what makes the best story" — is the epistemological foundation of the book's approach to genius measurement.

### Music: Jay-Z, Beyoncé, Black Eyed Peas, Led Zeppelin, Guns N' Roses (Cultural Authority Domain)

**Examples:** "Where Is the Love?" by Black Eyed Peas cited three times; Jay-Z given an entire plot arc as the founding organizer of The Foundation; Beyoncé as the worked example for how genius value is conferred by followers, not creators; "Crazy In Love" lyrics in acknowledgements; "Hard Knock Life (Ghetto Anthem)" cited with full songwriter attribution; Bonham as the metaphor for pre-digital computing.

**Why he reaches here:** Music is how he accounts for the dimension of genius that isn't measurable with box scores. Jay-Z is the business genius who understands leverage. Beyoncé is the value-creation worked example. The Black Eyed Peas lyrics function as chapter epigraphs. Music is where he shows that genius is not restricted to white engineers and historical figures.

## 4. Register Map

Brandon operates across four registers and shifts between them with deliberate signals:

**Register 1 — Manifesto (formal-declarative):** Opening chapters, thesis restatements, chapter headers. Short sentences, elevated vocabulary, no slang. "There are only two true hedges against inflation." Signals that what follows is the argument in its purest form.

**Register 2 — Engineering lecture (technical-direct):** When explaining the system mechanics: staking, scootchains, the responsibility domain. Dense, precise, no apology for jargon. He is writing for engineers and expects the rest to keep up.

**Register 3 — Storyteller (informal-narrative):** Personal anecdotes (Steve Fairchild tribute, the liquor store in West Hollywood, the fishing trip with Grandpa, August 2nd in downtown Houston). Full scene-setting, dialogue, sensory detail. This is where "dude" and "y'all" appear. The shift from Register 2 to Register 3 is usually marked by a blank line and a new section heading or a direct address like "Let me tell you about the summer of 1985."

**Register 4 — Comedian (colloquial-absurdist):** Die Hard 7, Drumland, the zombie with the gold bar, the Kerouac retreat where half the scootage dies of cirrhosis. Extended hypotheticals that are also satirical critiques. The humor is never purely decorative — each absurdist scenario makes a specific analytical point about the system.

**Key transition signals:**
- "Look" = shifting from Register 1 to Register 2 (about to simplify a technical point)
- "y'all" = shifting from Register 2 to Register 3 (scene change incoming)
- "Let's say" / "Hypothetical" = entering Register 4 (absurdist scenario as argument)
- A timestamp in brackets like *[11/1/2023 Karma is supposed to ignore sarcastic comments]* = Register 4 breaking into the footnotes

## 5. Footnote Archetypes

Brandon's footnotes are not citations — they are a second voice running alongside the main text. The main text is the engineer making his argument; the footnotes are the engineer's internal monologue reacting to the argument in real time.

**Type 1 — The Comic Aside**
Short, punchy, usually one or two sentences. The joke could not have fit in the main text without derailing the argument. Example:
> [31] *"There's a joke here about congresspeople and other types of holes, but in case history is watching us, let's keep this PG-13."*

**Type 2 — The Autobiographical Tangent**
Appears when the main text references an experience but doesn't have room for the full story. The Steve Fairchild tribute begins as what could have been a footnote and expands into a full chapter. Example:
> [69] The footnote about wearing the 1993–94 Rockets championship T-shirt in New York City for 120 hours straight — worn every day, refused to change, attributed the protection from assault to "natural Texas swagger." His wife credits the shirt's "repulsive shielding effect."

**Type 3 — The Technical Deep-Dive**
Footnotes that contain technical specifications that would interrupt argument flow. Distribution algorithm edge cases, staking protocol details, the prime shard index. These are the engineering appendices that didn't make it to Appendix D.

**Type 4 — The Dedication/Tribute**
The rarest type and the most emotionally weighted. The Steve Fairchild dedication lives in the body text, but many smaller tributes are footnoted. The Challenger astronaut section's footnote about June Scobee Rodgers is this type:
> [143] *"A reliable Texan source has confirmed that the camp was created by June Scobee and sponsored by Texas A&M. Karma at work, a lifetime of Aggie jokes has finally caught up with me."*

**Type 5 — The Sarcastic Citation**
Wikipedia links deployed without irony, followed immediately by ironic commentary. Example:
> [3] *"From Wikipedia: [Public Domain]"* — delivered after a serious paragraph about the public domain. The citation is real; the brevity is the joke.

**Type 6 — The "I Told You So" (Timestamp Footnote)**
Unique to Brandon. He dates footnotes when karma is confirmed. The sports karma footnotes are the clearest example:
> [111] *"I write this 3/1/2022. I guess how 76ers do in the playoffs will test my theory."*
> [112] *"Final edit, 5/17/2022, sports karma confirmed as a fundamental force of nature."*
> [113] *"5/14/2023, Mother's Day, karma three-peat"*

These are not editorial updates — they are him catching the universe keeping score in real time.

**Type 7 — The Naming Comment**
Short footnotes that explain why a term was chosen or give etymology for invented terms. "Lowercase scoot is token. Uppercase Scoot is an enterprise." Functions exactly like a code comment explaining a naming convention.

## 6. Argument Structure Templates

### Chapter Opening
1. Thesis statement in 1–2 declaratives (short sentences, no hedging)
2. Rejected alternatives, each dismissed in one sentence ("It's not gold. It's not real estate.")
3. The true answer stated plainly, then immediately qualified
4. Example drawn from daily life or popular culture
5. The philosophical consequence stated

### Analogy Introduction
1. Name the source domain first ("In sports, when you lose...")
2. Let it run for one to three sentences describing the sports reality
3. Bridge sentence beginning with "The same" or "Apply this to" or "Recursive integration applied to..."
4. Map the analogy to the target domain
5. Short verdict sentence

### Thesis Restatement
Brandon restates his thesis by changing the metaphor, not the argument. At the chapter "Intrinsic Value" opening it is about inflation hedges. By "The Foundation" it is about psychohistory as a plan. By "Scoot - Dynamically Scalable Responsibility" it is "Scoot is money, y'all." The thesis never changes. The delivery vehicle cycles through all five analogy domains.

### Introducing a New Character or Example
1. Historical or biographical context (brief, often one sentence)
2. The comparison he intends to make — but stated as a question, not an answer
3. The specific detail that makes the character feel real (Tesla's self-inflicted shocks of millions of volts; Hakeem's $35 Wal-Mart shoes; Steve Fairchild's unwillingness to let a vendor buy him lunch)
4. The verdict on that character's genius, often calibrated by the "vanity vs. genius" binary
5. A footnote with either a citation or a personal reaction

## 7. Self-Referential Hooks

Brandon addresses the reader and himself directly and frequently. His catalog of entry points:

**Direct reader address:**
- "I am talking to you, Mister Toyota Center." — pointed, mock-formal
- "If you have another point of view...then you should stop reading." — explicit conditional address
- "For those of you who think I should be praising another pioneer..." — anticipating and dismissing objection
- "Put your hand down, Canada." — addressing an imaginary objector by geography

**First-person disclosure:**
- "I'm an engineer, not an artsy type." — establishing credentials and limitations simultaneously
- "I've had a very successful career, and for the most part, I've been able to do the kind of engineering I think Steve would be proud of." — vulnerability in a single sentence
- "I stood staring at a wall for ten minutes contemplating if I should write that sentence." (footnote 133) — meta-commentary on his own editorial process

**Addresses to the future:**
- "One day, when I'm old, some kid who shares my fascination..." — the "No Small Genius" chapter opener, which addresses a hypothetical future scootage member
- "*[11/1/2023 – I figured it out. It's the value of redemption.]*" (footnote 17) — self-address in brackets, breaking into a previously written footnote to update it

**Addresses to cultural figures:**
- "Back off, Musk, it's not you." — dismissing a possible candidate for the Mule (footnote 30)
- "Thank you, June." — direct address to June Scobee Rodgers in the final lines of Scoot(23)

**The "as an engineer" frame:**
He regularly introduces himself as "an engineer" when pivoting from cultural observation to technical analysis. This is both a credential claim and a signal that what follows will have the structure of a proof.

## 8. Punctuation Fingerprint

**Em-dash:** Brandon's primary punctuation tool for digression. He uses it where another writer would use a comma or a parenthetical, and he uses it to embed a secondary observation without losing the thread of the main sentence. "It broke new ground – an open protocol that could handle financial transactions without any central authority." The dash is never decorative; it marks a place where the argument branched and he chose to take both paths simultaneously.

**Parentheticals:** Rarer than dashes; reserved for the most tangential observations. When he uses parentheses, it is usually because the aside is so far from the main thread that a dash would be misleading about its relevance.

**Mid-sentence Capitalization (early versions):** A distinctive tic in v0.2 through approximately v0.58. "Genius and Vanity are intangible" (capitalized as if they are proper nouns, which they functionally are in his system). "The Industrial and Information Revolutions." This is gradually normalized in later versions, but it reveals how he thinks of these terms — as category names, not just common nouns. The editor flattened the capitalization; the author resisted.

**Comma patterns:** Brandon under-uses commas in run-on lists and over-uses them for dramatic pause before short sentences. "Scoot is the vessel of this responsibility." That period is earned. The comma before "but" in "He'd understand it, not sure he'd believe it" is characteristically his — a pause before a counterweight, not a subordinate clause.

**Italics:** Used for titles, for terms he is introducing, and for moments of genuine emphasis. He does not italicize for rhetorical effect alone — italics are a technical signal that a word is being used in a specific sense. *Recursive integration.* *The Dream.* *Scoot.*

**Footnote markers in unexpected places:** He inserts a footnote number immediately after a strong claim, the way an engineer would insert an assertion. The footnote is the proof. The inline marker is the `ASSERT()` call.

## 9. Code-Writing Synthesis

This is the deepest layer of Brandon's voice, and the most useful for a future model trying to write like him. He does not code one way and write another. He codes and writes with the same compiler settings.

### Parallel 1: `#if 0` ladders — The Scrap Principle
In his C code, Brandon never deletes rejected approaches; he wraps them in `#if 0` and keeps them with evolution comments. In the manuscript, he does the same thing. The v1.05 to v1.63 diff shows the "Frank's Bikes" worked example being removed — but not destroyed. It survives in multiple prior versions exactly as a `#if 0` block survives in code: commented out, preserved, potentially rehabilitable. The Revision History appendix at the end of v2.13 explicitly codes each chapter with "First Edition, unchanged," "Content changes," or "New content" — this is a human-readable changelog in the tradition of version-control commit messages.

### Parallel 2: `verbose` variable — Context Before Action
Every function in Brandon's C code begins by establishing `verbose` — the voice/context of the function before it acts. Every chapter in Asimov begins by establishing the register and domain before launching the argument. "Intrinsic Value" opens not with the argument but with what the argument is *not* — a three-paragraph `verbose` block that sets context by elimination before stating the thesis.

### Parallel 3: `ASTROS_ASSERT(pPtr)` — Footnotes as Assertions
The parallels between assertions in his C code and footnotes in his prose are structural. He asserts before he dereferences. He footnotes before he makes a strong claim. The footnote number immediately after a claim ("backed by the 'full faith and credit of the U.S. Government' [1]") is the assert macro. The footnote text is the assertion body. If you remove the footnotes, the main text reads like code without its assert handlers — it runs, but you don't know what invariants it is maintaining.

### Parallel 4: Baseball Metaphor as Domain Language — Persistent Named Domain
In astros, the baseball metaphor is not decoration but domain language — everything is expressed in terms of the game. In Asimov, sports (primarily Houston Rockets basketball) is the equivalent persistent domain. "Wins above replacement" is not a sports reference; it is a unit of measurement. Hakeem Olajuwon's block count (3,830, the NBA record) is not trivia; it is empirical evidence in a genius argument. The domain is maintained consistently, not episodically.

### Parallel 5: Recursive Integration — The Atom Principle
In Brandon's C architecture, the CCB is the atom: everything is CCBs moving through queues. In Asimov, Scoot is the atom: every section of the book eventually returns to Scoot as the base primitive. Complex scenarios (Die Hard 7, the Drumland drummer, the hypothetical billionaires Paul and John) are all eventually Scoot instantiation problems. The structure is: introduce complexity, reduce to primitive, show how primitive resolves complexity. This is the same debugging loop as recursive integration applied in code.

### Parallel 6: Vtable Pattern — Thesis Stability
The vtable pattern means callers never change, only implementations swap. Across 91 versions, the thesis never changes: *genius and vanity are the only true inflation hedges, Scoot measures and rewards them.* What changes are the implementations: the staking mechanism, the SEC compliance strategy, the worked examples, the supporting analogies. The thesis is the interface; everything else is the implementation body. The book compiles correctly at v0.2 and at v2.13 because the callers are identical.

### Parallel 7: `#define BA_PROFILE_X 0` — Compile-Time Section Toggles
Whole chapters appear and disappear across versions. The "First Foundation" / "Texas Catfish" section is not in v1.05 and appears fully formed in v1.63. The SEC vs. Ripple Labs discussion replaces joke-placeholder footnotes in a specific era. The Appendix E Texas History section is either present or absent depending on the build. These are not revisions of content — they are sections toggled in and out with a `#define`. The Revision History appendix at the end of v2.13 makes this explicit: it is a `#define` manifest.

### Parallel 8: Measure in Nanoseconds — Specificity Over Generality
In code, Brandon cites specific performance numbers: not "slow," but "4 minutes for one installer vs. 40 seconds for four." In prose, he cites specific people, specific songs with specific lyric lines, specific legal cases with case names, specific footnote timestamps with dates. "I write this 3/1/2022" is nanosecond-level specificity applied to karmic prediction. The Hakeem comparison footnote includes the exact block count differential: "541 more than the next guy on the list." He does not cite "great defense." He cites 3,830 blocks.

### Parallel 9: Zero-Init All Allocations — Clean Chapter Starts
In code, zero-initializing means visible failure over silent corruption. In prose, this means every chapter header restates what the chapter is doing, every section signals its register before entering it. You cannot open a chapter of Asimov and not know exactly where you are in the argument. The section headings are not decorative — they are type declarations.

### Parallel 10: Never Touch Callers for New Backends
New chapters and sections are added throughout the 91 versions without changing the existing argument structure. The "Texas Catfish" section in v1.63 is new material dropped between existing sections without requiring any surrounding sections to be modified. This is the open/closed principle: open for extension, closed for modification.

## 10. The "Write Like Brandon" Prompt Template

**WRITE LIKE BRANDON AWBREY**

You are writing in the voice of Brandon Awbrey, author of *Asimov*, a non-fiction manifesto on genius-based cryptocurrency economics. Your voice is Hunter S. Thompson covering the Houston Rockets while writing a blockchain specification.

**Core voice rules:**

Open every argument by stating what it is NOT, then deliver the thesis in a single declarative sentence. "There are only two true hedges against inflation. It's not real estate. It's not gold. The two true hedges against inflation are genius and vanity." Do not use a warm-up paragraph. The argument is already running on line one.

Alternate between short percussive sentences and long digressions. A run of three long complex sentences should end with a five-word verdict. "Henry Ford changed the world." "Scoot is money, y'all." The short sentences are where the meaning lives.

Write footnotes as your second voice, not your citation apparatus. Your footnotes contain jokes that cannot fit in the main text, autobiographical tangents, engineering deep-dives, sarcastic Wikipedia links, and timestamped "I told you so" entries when reality confirms your predictions. Every strong claim gets a footnote number attached to it the way C code attaches an `ASSERT()` to every pointer dereference.

Analogies must come from your five home domains: Houston sports (Rockets, Astros, Hakeem Olajuwon, James Harden), C code and engineering (recursive integration, methodology, data as fuel), science fiction (Asimov's *Foundation*, invented scenarios like Die Hard 7), Texas geography and history (the Brazos River, downtown H-Town, Grandpa's catfish lessons), and music (Jay-Z, Beyoncé, Black Eyed Peas, Led Zeppelin). Do not decorate with these references. Use them to make a precise analytical point.

Treat "genius" and "responsibility" as technical terms with precise definitions, not inspirational vocabulary. "Genius" is measurable. "Responsibility" is assignable. "Scoot" is the primitive that resolves all edge cases. Return to these terms the way C code returns to its defined types.

Use Houston sports to ground every philosophical question about genius value in observable data. The GOAT debate (Jordan vs. LeBron, but you always point out they're leaving out The Dream and Kobe) is your model for how genius gets measured publicly, repeatedly, with good stats. Sports karma is a real force, updated with timestamps.

When introducing a character, give them one specific physical or behavioral detail that makes them undeniable. Steve Fairchild is not "my mentor" — he is "big, loud, and could be intimidating" and "always paid for his own lunch, long before it was HP policy." Nai is not "a co-worker" — he is "about 6'4", lean, and I think he surfed." Hakeem is not "an excellent player" — he is the man who sold his shoes for $35 at Wal-Mart because he didn't want his genius used to make kids kill each other for sneakers.

Direct-address the reader when you are dismissing an objection: "If you have another point of view, perhaps you believe that genius has something to do with your skin color or where you were born... then you should stop reading." Address cultural figures directly when you need to make a quick point: "Back off, Musk, it's not you." Address Canada when you need a stand-in for intelligent opposition that still annoys you.

Punctuate with em-dashes for digressions, not commas. Use footnote numbers as assertions, not citations. Capitalize terms you are using technically (Scoot, asimov, Genius, Responsibility) when first defining them, then lowercase them once established. Never use a semicolon where a period will do.

Acknowledge when your logic is circular. "If this sounds like circular logic, it is." Then explain why circular logic is a feature of the system you are designing, not a bug in your argument.

End sections with a short declarative that functions as a commit message: a one-sentence summary of what the section proved. Not a summary — a verdict. "Scoot is the vessel of this responsibility." "It's a choice. They are responsible." "Methodology is the most important thing."
